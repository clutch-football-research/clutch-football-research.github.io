Can You Measure Clutch?

What 4,531 NFL late-game opportunities tell us about quarterback performance under pressure — and why no single statistic can answer the question by itself.

“Clutch” might be the most frequently used and least precisely defined word in football.

A quarterback leads a game-winning drive and he was clutch. He throws an interception with two minutes left and he wasn’t. His team loses after he gives them the lead and somehow that can still become evidence that he failed in the fourth quarter.

We know clutch when we see it. Measuring it is much harder.

That was the problem behind this study.

The goal was not to discover a mystical clutch gene or create a statistic capable of declaring, with certainty, that one quarterback possesses something another does not. Football is too dependent on teammates, field position, time, score, coaching decisions and opponent quality for that.

The more realistic objective was to ask whether clutch quarterback performance could be measured in a way that was consistent, repeatable and less dependent on what happened after the quarterback left the field.

After examining 4,557 late-game opportunities across NFL seasons from 1999 through the current 2026 research snapshot, the answer appears to be yes — with an important qualification.

There probably is no single clutch statistic.

But there may be a much better framework for measuring clutch performance.

What is already being measured?

Football already has well-established ways of describing late-game quarterback success.

Fourth-quarter comebacks, or 4QC, capture games in which a team comes back from a fourth-quarter deficit and wins.

Game-winning drives, or GWD, identify late possessions that ultimately produce the decisive lead.

More sophisticated work, particularly Scott Kacsmar's research, has gone considerably further by examining clutch opportunities, late-game drives and adjustments for situations in which quarterbacks appear to do their jobs but the team ultimately loses.

Those statistics are valuable. Our results don't suggest otherwise.

But most of them begin, in one form or another, with the outcome of the game.

That creates an attribution problem.

Imagine a quarterback takes possession late in the fourth quarter trailing by four. He drives 75 yards for a touchdown and gives his team the lead.

The opponent gets the ball back and scores.

His team loses.

From the standpoint of the scoreboard, the quarterback lost the game. From the standpoint of the opportunity he was given, his offense succeeded.

The reverse can happen too. A quarterback can fail on a late possession, get the ball back because his defense creates another opportunity, and ultimately win.

Traditional clutch statistics are good at answering questions such as, “How often did the quarterback's team ultimately win from this late-game situation?”

We wanted to isolate a slightly different question: did the quarterback's offense convert when the game was still in its hands?

That difference sounds small. Across thousands of games, it isn't.

Defining a clutch opportunity

The foundation of the framework is Clutch Drive Conversion Rate, or CDCR.

Instead of counting every fourth-quarter drive or beginning with the final score, CDCR selects one final viable opportunity for each team in a game.

The possession must begin in the fourth quarter or overtime with the offense tied or trailing by no more than eight points.

There is also a viability test. A possession must have at least a 5 percent modeled probability of reaching the field position necessary to create the required scoring threat.

That prevents a possession beginning at a team's own 10-yard line with only a few seconds remaining from being treated the same as a legitimate final opportunity.

Once the opportunity is identified, the question is straightforward.

If the game is tied, take the lead. If trailing by one through six, tie the game or take the lead. If trailing by seven, score the touchdown. If trailing by eight, produce the touchdown and two-point conversion necessary to tie the game.

Whatever happens afterward is recorded separately.

That is the fundamental difference between CDCR and an outcome-based clutch statistic. CDCR stops grading the quarterback when the selected offensive opportunity ends.

The difference is not theoretical

We reconstructed a Kacsmar-style game-outcome framework over the same quarterback cohort so we could compare the approaches directly.

The measures are related. They should be.

The Spearman rank correlation between the raw game-outcome measure and CDCR is about 0.62.

But that is nowhere close to saying the two measures are interchangeable.

Among the 64 quarterbacks in the common qualified cohort, 28 move at least 10 ranking positions, 15 move at least 20, and 4 move at least 30. The average quarterback moves about 11.6 ranking places.

Some of those movements are enormous.

Joe Burrow moves from 49th in the raw game-outcome ranking to seventh in CDCR. Justin Herbert moves from 41st to fifth. Rich Gannon moves from 53rd to 20th.

In the opposite direction, Alex Smith moves from 26th to 62nd, Deshaun Watson from 20th to 52nd, Donovan McNabb from 29th to 59th and Russell Wilson from 24th to 50th.

Those differences are not evidence that the traditional framework is wrong. They show that it is measuring something different.

A game-outcome statistic includes what happened after the quarterback's opportunity. CDCR deliberately does not.

The adjusted Kacsmar-style framework narrows some of that disagreement by accounting for additional context, which is important. In Burrow's case, for example, the adjusted ranking moves dramatically closer to CDCR.

That is evidence that both approaches are recognizing the same underlying attribution problem.

CDCR's distinction is that the separation is built into the unit of measurement itself: one final viable offensive opportunity, evaluated independently of the eventual team outcome.

How CDCR compares with other clutch measures

Several established football analytics approaches recognize the same basic problem: a quarterback's late-game performance can be obscured by what teammates, special teams or the opponent do around him. Where the methods differ is in the unit they choose to measure.

The overlap in results is partial, which is what we would hope to see if the framework is measuring something real but not simply reproducing another leaderboard. Patrick Mahomes remains elite across many approaches. But other quarterbacks move substantially depending on the question being asked.

For example, CBS Sports ranked Josh Allen fourth, Daniel Jones eighth and Lamar Jackson ninth in its 2024 clutch ranking. In the current CDCR cohort, their raw CDCR ranks are 14th, 48th and 37th; their sCDCR point-estimate ranks are 36th, 49th and 39th. Justin Herbert moves the other direction: CBS ranked him 10th, while he is fifth in raw CDCR.

The point is not that one publication is wrong. The disagreement is evidence that these methods are measuring different aspects of late-game quarterback performance. CDCR is designed to answer a narrower question: when the offense reached its final realistic chance to change the game, did it convert — and how difficult was that opportunity?

The difficulty problem

A possession starting tied at midfield with four minutes remaining is not equivalent to one beginning at your own 15 while trailing by six with 50 seconds left.

Raw CDCR treats both as attempts. That is intentional — the first question is simply whether the opportunity converted.

But if the goal is to compare quarterbacks, difficulty has to enter the analysis.

That is why we created Expected Clutch Drive Conversion Rate, or xCDCR.

xCDCR estimates how often an opportunity should be converted based on its starting conditions.

From there we calculate Clutch Above Expected, or CAE:

CAE = actual CDCR − expected CDCR

Steve McNair leads the qualified cohort at approximately 16.5 percentage points above expectation. Eli Manning is second at 15.7. Tom Brady is third at 14.7. Peyton Manning is fourth at 14.4. Patrick Mahomes is fifth at 14.0.

Eli Manning is perhaps the clearest example of why this matters. His 53.6 percent CDCR is good, but it ranks only 10th. His expected conversion rate is just 37.9 percent. Once difficulty is considered, his performance becomes one of the strongest in the dataset.

On trailing opportunities specifically, Eli ranks first in CAE.

Putting quarterbacks on the same playing field

Difficulty solves one problem. Sample size creates another.

A quarterback with 27 opportunities should not be treated as though we know as much about him as one with 83.

Standardized Clutch Drive Conversion Rate, or sCDCR, addresses that issue.

It estimates the quarterback effect with partial pooling and then evaluates quarterbacks over a common distribution of clutch opportunities.

The top of that leaderboard is Tom Brady, Eli Manning, Peyton Manning, Ben Roethlisberger, Drew Brees, Derek Carr, Patrick Mahomes and Steve McNair.

Once again, the exact ranking is less interesting than the consistency.

Brady is sixth in raw CDCR, third in CAE and first in sCDCR. Peyton Manning is fourth, fourth and third. Roethlisberger is eighth, sixth and fourth. Brees is third, eighth and fifth. Mahomes is first, fifth and seventh.

Those quarterbacks remain strong as we progressively remove different sources of distortion.

That is the closest this study gets to identifying a convincing “clutch profile.”

So which quarterbacks look most clutch?

This is where we have to be careful.

There is no statistical threshold that suddenly turns a quarterback into a clutch quarterback.

But the framework does allow us to ask a more defensible question: which quarterbacks continue to look exceptional regardless of how we examine their late-game opportunities?

Tom Brady has one of the strongest cases in the dataset. His raw CDCR is 56.6% across 83 resolved opportunities, despite an expected conversion rate of only 41.7%. He ranks #2 in CAE and #1 in sCDCR.

Peyton Manning remains near the top under all three measures. Ben Roethlisberger does too. Drew Brees remains strong across a very large sample. Patrick Mahomes owns the highest raw conversion rate and remains fifth above expected even after the difficulty of his opportunities is considered.

Eli Manning may be the most interesting discovery. His raw CDCR does not immediately separate him from the group, but difficulty adjustment and standardization do. He ranks second overall in both CAE and sCDCR, and first in both measures when examining trailing opportunities.

That doesn't prove that any of them possess an immutable psychological trait called clutch.

It does show that their historical late-game performance remains unusually strong after several obvious alternative explanations are removed.

When the measures disagree

Disagreement is just as informative.

Joe Burrow is a useful example.

His raw CDCR is 53.8%, #10 in the cohort.

But his expected CDCR is also approximately 54.1%. His overall CAE is essentially zero, and his standardized rank falls to #39.

That doesn't mean Burrow is “not clutch.”

Burrow’s movement from #7 in raw CDCR to #38 in CAE and sCDCR is not caused by Cincinnati’s defense or by the team’s final record.

His raw CDCR is 56.0%: 14 conversions in 25 final viable opportunities. But those opportunities also carried an average expected conversion rate of about 56.0%. In other words, he converted almost exactly as often as the difficulty of his situations predicted. That leaves his CAE essentially at zero, which ranks 38th.

sCDCR then adds sample-size shrinkage and places every quarterback on the same opportunity distribution. Burrow’s standardized estimate is 43.6%, also 38th by point estimate.

That is very different from the Kacsmar-style raw outcome ranking, where Burrow is much lower because the final team result matters. In fact, once that framework is adjusted for situations such as the defense surrendering the lead after Burrow’s offense succeeded, his rank rises dramatically.

So the distinction is simple: Burrow’s low raw outcome rank can be affected by what happened after his drive. His CAE and sCDCR ranks cannot.

It means the evidence supporting exceptional clutch performance is considerably weaker than the raw conversion rate makes it appear.

Justin Herbert presents another version of the same lesson. He ranks #9 in raw CDCR but #18 in CAE and #20 in sCDCR.

A useful clutch framework should be capable of both discovering overlooked performance and tempering results that initially look extraordinary.

Is the final opportunity really different?

There is an obvious objection to the entire approach: why should the final meaningful opportunity receive special treatment?

NFL teams themselves provide some evidence.

On earlier qualifying fourth-quarter possessions, teams facing fourth down went for it approximately 17.7 percent of the time. On the terminal opportunity, that number jumps to 56.9 percent.

Punting moves in the opposite direction. Teams punt on approximately 61.2 percent of earlier qualifying fourth-down situations but only about 17.7 percent on terminal opportunities.

Across more than 3,300 same-team, same-game comparisons, terminal possessions are also substantially more likely to include a fourth-down attempt and more likely to achieve the score necessary to tie or take the lead.

Teams behave as though the final opportunity is different. When another possession is unlikely to exist, strategy changes.

The 2021 AFC Divisional Game

The 2021 AFC Divisional Game between Buffalo and Kansas City exposed a different measurement problem.

Josh Allen led Buffalo to a touchdown with 13 seconds remaining. Patrick Mahomes answered, moving Kansas City into field-goal range to tie the game. Mahomes then led another successful possession in overtime.

A one-opportunity-per-team metric cannot fully describe what happened.

Allen delivered under pressure. Mahomes delivered under pressure. Then Mahomes delivered again.

That raised an obvious question: why not simply keep every successful late-game drive?

We tested that idea. It produced a statistical problem.

If earlier successes remain in the dataset while earlier failures can disappear because a later opportunity supersedes them, the denominator becomes dependent on the outcome. Successes and failures are no longer being treated symmetrically.

That makes the resulting percentage unsuitable as a conversion rate.

So we kept CDCR's one-final-opportunity structure. But the Buffalo–Kansas City example had exposed something real that we did not want to throw away.

That led to Clutch Response Events.

A Clutch Response Event preserves a successful qualifying late-game response even when a later possession becomes the team's final CDCR opportunity.

It is deliberately a count, not another percentage.

Across the study, we identified 4,194 successful Clutch Response Events.

The implication is important: clutch performance is not necessarily zero-sum just because winning is.

Two quarterbacks can both perform successfully under pressure. The scoreboard still has to declare one of them the loser.

Are we actually measuring clutch better?

That is ultimately the question.

The answer is not that CDCR has finally solved clutch. It hasn't.

But the evidence suggests this framework does improve the measurement problem in several important ways.

Fourth-quarter comebacks and game-winning drives tell us something meaningful about team outcomes.

Kacsmar-style adjusted approaches go further by recognizing situations in which conventional outcomes can misrepresent quarterback performance.

CDCR changes the unit of analysis itself, isolating one final viable opportunity and stopping quarterback attribution when that offensive opportunity ends.

xCDCR adds the difficulty of the situation. CAE asks whether the quarterback exceeded that difficulty. sCDCR accounts for opportunity mix and limited samples. Clutch Response Events preserve successful pressure responses that would otherwise disappear from a one-opportunity metric.

No single one of those measures identifies “clutch.” Together, however, they give us a much clearer picture of clutch performance.

There is one final limitation that matters.

sCDCR point rankings should be read as estimates rather than exact ordinal positions. In the current sensitivity check, changing the reference opportunity population produced nearly identical trailing rankings (Spearman rho = 1.00000). Held-out predictive checks are mixed, so the site does not claim that sCDCR improves prediction. Full scientific audit results remain available in the downloadable publication metadata.

The scoped held-out predictive comparison is not supported on the predeclared held-out scores. See the machine-readable sensitivity results for both scores.

The core metrics describe historical opportunities. The disclosed sCDCR validation results determine its publication role; they do not establish predictive improvement.

That may be an important distinction between measuring clutch and proving the existence of a permanent clutch trait.

The framework cannot tell us with certainty who “is clutch.”

But it can tell us much more clearly what quarterbacks have actually done when the game was in their hands.

And that moves the conversation from reputation toward evidence.
